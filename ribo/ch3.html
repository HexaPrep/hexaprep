3.1 Insurance Industry Structure

Who the players are

Before a broker can explain a policy or a claim, it helps to know who does what across the industry. Each role below has a distinct function, even though clients often lump them all together as "the insurance company."

RoleFunction
InsurersThe companies that actually accept risk, collect premium, and pay claims. They design products, set rates (subject to regulatory approval where applicable), and hold the financial responsibility for covered losses.
BrokersRIBO-licensed intermediaries who represent the client, can access multiple insurers, and advise on coverage suited to the client's needs (see 3.2 for the full distribution comparison).
AgentsIntermediaries who typically represent one insurer (or a limited set) rather than shopping the broader market on the client's behalf.
Direct writersInsurers that sell directly to the public without an independent broker or agent intermediary — the insurer's own employees or online channel serve the client directly.
Managing General Agents (MGAs)Specialized intermediary organizations that insurers delegate certain underwriting, binding, or program-management authority to — often used to access specialty or harder-to-place markets. Brokers may interact with MGAs as a route into markets not otherwise directly accessible.
UnderwritersThe insurer professionals who evaluate risk, decide whether to accept it, and determine pricing and terms.
Claims adjustersThe professionals (insurer staff or independent) who investigate and evaluate claims and determine the amount payable under the policy.
Clients/insuredsThe individuals or businesses who purchase coverage and are protected by the policy.
RegulatorsRIBO, FSRA, and other bodies covered in Chapter 1, overseeing licensing, market conduct, and compliance.
Industry associationsOrganizations such as IBAO (Insurance Brokers Association of Ontario) that support, represent, and provide resources to the broker community (see 3.12, 12.4).
Exam Tip A common exam pattern names a role in a scenario ("the person who evaluates your claim and determines the payout is the ___") and asks you to identify it. Know the function of each role well enough to identify it from a description, not just from its name.

3.2 Distribution Methods

Broker vs. agent vs. direct insurer

This comparison is one of the most heavily tested distinctions in the general insurance content domain.

Who they represent: the client. A broker owes duties of advice and advocacy to the person buying the insurance.

Market access: typically able to place business with multiple insurers, allowing genuine comparison shopping and market-fit selection.

Client relationship: ongoing, advisory — the broker is expected to identify needs, compare markets, and recommend appropriate coverage (see Chapter 9).

Compensation: generally commission-based, paid by the insurer, though this can create the conflict-of-interest considerations covered in Chapter 2.

Responsibilities: licensed and regulated by RIBO in Ontario; carries the professional duty of care and E&O exposure discussed in 1.3 and 8.6-8.7.

Who they represent: typically the insurer (or a specific, limited set of insurers) rather than the client broadly.

Market access: generally limited to the insurer(s) they're appointed to represent.

Client relationship: can still be advisory, but within the bounds of what their appointing insurer(s) offer.

Responsibilities: subject to applicable licensing requirements for their role, distinct from a broker's RIBO licence and multi-market obligations.

Who they represent: the insurer itself — there is no independent intermediary in the relationship.

Market access: the client only accesses that one insurer's products, with no market comparison built into the transaction.

Client relationship: often more transactional/self-directed (e.g., online purchase), without the same individualized advisory relationship a broker provides.

RIBO Exam Trap "Broker vs. agent" is a named confusion pair in the official exam trap list. The core distinction to hold onto: a broker represents the client and can typically access multiple markets; an agent typically represents the insurer and is limited to what that insurer offers.

3.3 Insurance Company Structures

How insurers themselves are organized

Stock (company) insurers
Owned by shareholders, who share in profits; the insurer operates to generate a return for its shareholders in addition to paying claims.
Mutual insurers
Owned by their policyholders rather than outside shareholders; profits/surplus generally benefit the policyholder-owners rather than external investors.
MGAs
Not insurers themselves, but delegated underwriting/program managers acting on an insurer's behalf (see 3.1) — included here because they're a recurring structural feature of how certain markets are accessed.
Lloyd's
A marketplace of syndicates rather than a single insurance company (see 3.4 for full treatment).
Syndicates
Groups of underwriting members that come together within the Lloyd's marketplace to underwrite specific risks.
Important A stock insurer and a mutual insurer can offer functionally similar policies to a client — the difference is in ownership and governance, not necessarily in coverage quality. Don't assume one structure is inherently "better" for the client; the structural distinction is a knowledge-level exam point, not a client-advisory recommendation.

3.4 Lloyd's of London

What Lloyd's is

Lloyd's of London is not a single insurance company — it is a marketplace where multiple underwriting syndicates come together to underwrite risk. Each syndicate is made up of underwriting members who provide capital and share in the risk (and potential profit) of the policies the syndicate writes.

HOW IT WORKS

A risk brought to Lloyd's is typically presented through a broker with access to the Lloyd's market. One or more syndicates may agree to underwrite portions of that risk — meaning a single Lloyd's policy can actually be backed by several different syndicates sharing the exposure, similar in concept to the subscription policies covered in Chapter 6.

SPECIALTY RISKS

Lloyd's is particularly known for underwriting unusual, large, or highly specialized risks that standard markets may not have the appetite or expertise to write — historically including marine, aviation, and other niche or emerging risk categories.

BROKER INTERACTION

An Ontario broker does not typically access Lloyd's directly — access is usually arranged through a broker with specific Lloyd's market access or through an MGA/wholesale relationship, given the specialized nature of the marketplace.

Exam Tip The key knowledge-level fact to retain: Lloyd's is a marketplace of syndicates, not a single insurer. If an exam question asks "which of the following best describes Lloyd's of London," resist any answer that frames it as a single traditional insurance company.

3.5 Facility Association

Purpose and why it exists

The Facility Association is a residual market mechanism for automobile insurance in Ontario (and other provinces). It exists specifically because automobile insurance is compulsory (see 1.9, 4.1) — every eligible driver needs a route to coverage, even those whose risk profile falls outside what standard insurers are willing to write on a voluntary basis.

ELIGIBILITY AND HOW IT OPERATES

Drivers who cannot obtain standard automobile insurance in the voluntary market — due to factors like driving record, claims history, or other underwriting concerns — may be eligible for coverage through the Facility Association. Member insurers share in the results (premiums and losses) of this residual pool, rather than any single insurer bearing the full cost of insuring higher-risk drivers alone.

RELATIONSHIP TO THE TAKE ALL COMERS RULE

The Facility Association is the practical mechanism that makes the Take All Comers Rule (see 1.9) workable in practice: rather than forcing every individual insurer to accept every risk on standard terms, drivers outside standard market appetite have a designated residual market path to compulsory coverage.

Quick check: does the Facility Association replace the Take All Comers Rule?

No — they work together. The Take All Comers Rule requires insurers to accept qualified applicants within their own approved underwriting criteria. For drivers who fall outside any standard insurer's appetite, the Facility Association provides the residual-market path to obtaining the compulsory coverage Ontario law requires.

Important A broker's role when a client doesn't fit standard markets is to identify the appropriate market path — which may include Facility Association access — rather than simply declining to help or, worse, misrepresenting information to force a standard-market fit (see the Chapter 1 scenario on this same issue).

Service. The ongoing relationship during the policy period — mid-term changes, questions, endorsements, and updates as the client's circumstances evolve (see 9.2, 9.3). Broker's role: respond to service requests and identify material changes that need reporting to the insurer.
Renewal. The policy comes up for its next term — information is reviewed and updated, and the client re-confirms or adjusts coverage (see 9.16). Broker's role: proactively review the account rather than treating renewal as automatic.
Claim. A loss occurs and the client reports it. Broker's role: assist with first notice of loss, explain the process, and manage expectations without overstepping into the insurer's adjusting role (see 10.6-10.7).
Settlement. The claim is resolved — paid, denied, or disputed, based on the coverage evaluation. Broker's role: help the client understand the outcome and, where a dispute arises, understand the appropriate resolution path (see 10.12).
Exam Tip Scenario questions often describe an event ("the underwriter requests additional information about the roof age") and ask which lifecycle stage it belongs to. Practice identifying the stage from a described activity, not just reciting the stage names in order.

3.7 Binding Authority

What binding means and why it matters

Binding is the moment coverage takes legal effect — the insurer is officially on risk from the bound date/time forward, even before the formal policy document is issued. Understanding binding authority matters because acting outside it can leave a client mistakenly believing they have coverage when they don't.

ConceptWhat it means
Broker authorityThe scope within which a broker can commit an insurer to coverage without needing case-by-case insurer approval — this varies by insurer, broker relationship, and type of risk.
Insurer authorityUltimately, the insurer defines the limits of what a broker (or MGA) can bind on its behalf — brokers cannot create coverage the insurer hasn't authorized.
ConditionsBinding is often conditional — e.g., subject to receipt of a satisfactory application, inspection, or specific underwriting information; coverage can be conditional pending confirmation of those requirements.
Differences between marketsBinding authority is not uniform — some markets grant brokers broad authority to bind directly; others (e.g., certain specialty or Lloyd's-accessed risks) require insurer/underwriter confirmation before binding is effective.
Warning — exceeding binding authority If a broker tells a client "you're covered" when the broker did not actually have the authority to bind that risk (or a required condition wasn't met), the client may believe they have coverage that doesn't actually exist. This is a serious E&O exposure (see 8.6) — confirm binding authority and conditions before confirming coverage to a client.

3.8 Loss Ratio

Definition and calculation

The loss ratio is a core metric insurers use to monitor the profitability and sustainability of a book of business. It compares claims paid against premium collected.

Loss Ratio = (Incurred Losses ÷ Earned Premium) × 100%
Enter both values to calculate.

WHY INSURERS MONITOR IT

A loss ratio tells an insurer roughly how much of every premium dollar is going back out in claims. A loss ratio consistently above what the insurer's pricing assumed signals the book of business is unprofitable at current rates.

RELATIONSHIP TO PRICING AND UNDERWRITING

A high or worsening loss ratio in a particular line, territory, or class of business typically leads insurers to raise rates, tighten underwriting criteria, or reduce their appetite for that class — directly affecting what's available to brokers and clients in that market going forward (see 3.9).

Exam Tip Loss ratio calculation questions are common. Keep the formula simple: losses ÷ premium, expressed as a percentage. A loss ratio over 100% means the insurer paid out more in claims than it collected in premium for that period — an unsustainable result if it continues.

3.9 Insurance Pricing

Factors affecting availability and pricing

Insurance pricing and market availability respond to a wide range of factors, many of which are outside any individual broker's or client's control. Understanding these helps a broker explain rate changes to clients in an informed, non-defensive way.

Inflation & building costs
Rising costs to repair or rebuild property directly increase the cost of claims, which feeds into pricing.
Claims frequency & severity
How often claims occur, and how large they are on average, both drive loss ratios and pricing.
Climate change & water losses
Increasing frequency of severe weather and water-related claims has materially affected habitational pricing and availability in recent years (see 5.8).
Natural disasters & secondary perils
Large-scale catastrophic events affect insurer capital and reinsurance costs, which flow through to pricing broadly.
Global events
Economic, geopolitical, and supply-chain events can affect claims costs (e.g., parts availability for auto claims) and reinsurance markets.
Technology
Can both increase costs (e.g., complex vehicle repair technology) and create efficiencies that offset some cost pressure.
Fraud
Fraudulent claims directly increase claims costs across the pool, raising premiums for all policyholders (see 1.10, 8.11).
Reinsurance environment
The cost insurers themselves pay to transfer part of their risk to reinsurers affects what insurers must charge policyholders.
Risk characteristics
The specific attributes of an individual risk (construction, location, driving record, claims history) remain the most direct pricing factor at the individual policy level.
Remember When a client is frustrated by a rate increase, being able to explain the broader pricing environment (not just "the insurer decided this") helps set realistic expectations and preserves trust — this connects directly to the difficult-conversations skills in Chapter 10.

3.10 Policy Structure

How to read a policy

Every insurance policy — auto, home, or commercial — is built from the same structural components. Learning to locate each one is a foundational broker skill tested throughout the exam.

Declarations▸

The page(s) at the front of the policy identifying the specific details of this policy: named insured, policy period, described property/vehicle, limits selected, deductibles, premium, and the specific coverages and endorsements that apply to this particular policyholder.

Where to find information: Always start here to confirm who is insured, what is insured, and the policy period before looking anywhere else.

Insuring agreements▸

The core promise of the policy — the section stating what the insurer agrees to do (e.g., "we will pay for direct physical loss to covered property caused by a covered peril"). This is where coverage is affirmatively granted.

Definitions▸

Policies define key terms precisely, and those definitions often differ from everyday usage. A word like "insured," "residence premises," or "accident" can have a specific, sometimes narrower or broader, meaning within the policy than in ordinary conversation — always check the definitions section rather than assuming plain-English meaning applies.

Conditions▸

Requirements the insured must meet for coverage to apply or continue — such as duties after a loss, cooperation requirements, or premium payment terms (see also statutory conditions, 4.12).

Exclusions▸

Specific causes, situations, or types of loss the policy states are not covered, even though they might otherwise seem to fall within the insuring agreement.

Extensions▸

Provisions built into the base policy that extend coverage slightly beyond the main insuring agreement — often with their own, smaller sub-limits.

Endorsements▸

Separate documents attached to the policy that modify its original terms — adding, removing, or changing coverage from what the base policy provides.

Limits and deductibles▸

Found in the declarations for the specific policy, but the underlying rules for how they apply (per occurrence, aggregate, per item) are often detailed further within the policy wording itself.

Statutory conditions (where applicable)▸

Certain policy types (notably automobile — see 4.12) are required by law to include specific statutory conditions, which cannot be removed or weakened by the insurer even if not restated verbatim in every policy document.

Exam Tip A recurring question format gives you a short policy excerpt and asks you to identify which structural component it represents (declarations vs. insuring agreement vs. exclusion, etc.). Practice this pattern-recognition skill directly — see the Policy Wording Exercise in the end matter below.

3.11 Policy Interpretation

The interpretation checklist

Reading a policy structurally (3.10) is the first step. Applying it to an actual claim or coverage question requires working through a consistent set of questions, in order:

  1. Who is insured? Check the declarations and definitions — is this specific person/entity actually a named insured, an additional insured, or not insured at all under this policy (see 9.4)?
  2. What property is insured? Confirm the specific property, vehicle, or exposure actually described falls within what the policy covers.
  3. What peril caused the loss? Identify the actual cause of loss (see 1.1) before assessing coverage.
  4. Is the loss covered? Check whether that peril falls within the insuring agreement.
  5. Is there an exclusion? Check whether an exclusion removes what would otherwise be covered.
  6. Is there an endorsement? Check whether an endorsement changes the standard exclusion or coverage position for this specific policy.
  7. What limit applies? Confirm the maximum payable for this type of loss.
  8. What deductible applies? Confirm what the insured must absorb before payment.
  9. What conditions apply? Confirm the insured has met (or can still meet) required conditions, such as timely notice.
Important This is the same sequence introduced in 1.1 for how insurance responds to a loss — 3.11 is where you formally practise applying it as a repeatable skill, rather than treating each step as a one-off fact.

APPLIED EXAMPLE

Example A tenant's policy covers their personal property against named perils including fire and theft. A guest's phone (not the tenant's own property) is stolen from the tenant's apartment. Working through the checklist: Who is insured? The tenant, under their own policy — the guest is not an insured. What property is insured? The tenant's own personal property, not a guest's belongings (unless a specific extension applies). Result: without a specific endorsement extending coverage to guests' property, this loss likely falls outside the tenant's own coverage, regardless of the fact that theft is a covered peril for the tenant's own belongings.

✓ Chapter 3 Knowledge Checks

Quick checks across this chapter's core concepts. Select an answer to see feedback.

1. A client wants to compare quotes from several different insurers through one relationship. Which distribution channel are they most likely using?
Correct answer: A broker. Brokers represent the client and typically have access to multiple insurers, enabling genuine market comparison — unlike an agent (typically tied to one insurer) or a direct writer (only their own products).
2. An insurer's incurred losses for the year are $720,000 and earned premium is $900,000. What is the loss ratio?
Correct answer: 80%. ($720,000 ÷ $900,000) × 100% = 80%.
3. A homeowner's policy excludes "water damage" generally, but an endorsement has been added extending coverage for sewer backup. A sewer backup loss occurs. Which policy structure component determines the actual outcome?
Correct answer: The endorsement. Endorsements modify the base policy's terms — here, adding back coverage the base exclusion would otherwise remove. Interpretation requires checking whether an endorsement changes the standard position (step 6 of the interpretation checklist).

Key Takeaways

  • The industry involves distinct roles — insurers, brokers, agents, direct writers, MGAs, underwriters, adjusters, clients, regulators, and industry associations — each with a specific function.
  • Brokers represent the client and typically access multiple insurers; agents typically represent one insurer; direct writers sell without an intermediary at all.
  • Insurers are structured as stock companies (shareholder-owned) or mutuals (policyholder-owned); Lloyd's is a marketplace of syndicates, not a single company.
  • The Facility Association is the residual market mechanism that makes compulsory auto insurance and the Take All Comers Rule workable for drivers outside standard market appetite.
  • The insurance lifecycle runs Risk → Application → Underwriting → Quote → Binding → Policy Issuance → Service → Renewal → Claim → Settlement, with defined broker, insurer, underwriter, adjuster, and client roles at each stage.
  • Binding authority varies by insurer and market — brokers must know the limits of their own authority and any conditions attached to binding before confirming coverage to a client.
  • Loss ratio (incurred losses ÷ earned premium) is a core profitability metric that directly drives pricing and underwriting appetite changes.
  • Pricing responds to inflation, claims frequency/severity, climate change, natural disasters, global events, technology, fraud, the reinsurance environment, and individual risk characteristics.
  • Every policy shares the same structural components: declarations, insuring agreements, definitions, conditions, exclusions, extensions, endorsements, limits, deductibles, and (where applicable) statutory conditions.
  • Policy interpretation follows a repeatable sequence: who's insured → what's insured → what peril → is it covered → exclusions → endorsements → limit → deductible → conditions.
  • Brokers rely on insurer portals, policy wordings, underwriting/rate manuals, bulletins, the Insurance Institute, IBAO, and regulators — but the actual issued policy wording always governs over general guidance.

Correct answer: B. ($1,560,000 ÷ $2,400,000) × 100% = 65%. This is the correct calculation and interpretation. Option C overreaches — a loss ratio alone doesn't automatically dictate a rate decrease, since expenses and other factors also affect pricing decisions.
APPLICATION · SCENARIO · MODERATE3. A broker confirms coverage is bound for a commercial risk that actually required underwriter sign-off before binding — sign-off that was never obtained. A loss occurs before sign-off is received. What is the most accurate description of this situation?
Correct answer: B. Binding authority has real limits, and exceeding it — confirming coverage that wasn't actually authorized — creates serious E&O exposure and leaves genuine doubt about whether coverage was in force at the time of loss. This is squarely the broker's responsibility to get right, not the client's.
APPLICATION · POLICY INTERPRETATION · DIFFICULT4. A named insured's policy covers "the described dwelling." Their adult child, who does not live in the home and is not listed in the declarations, asks whether damage to their own separate apartment would be covered under the parent's homeowners policy. Applying the interpretation checklist, what is the correct first question to resolve this?
Correct answer: C. The interpretation checklist starts with confirming who is insured and what property is actually insured. Here, the adult child's separate apartment is neither the named insured's described dwelling nor (based on the facts given) covered property — this threshold question resolves the issue before reaching peril, exclusions, or endorsements.

Correct answer: C. A broker represents the client and typically has multi-insurer market access, enabling comparison shopping within one advisory relationship — the defining feature that distinguishes brokers from agents and direct writers.
