2.1 Professionalism

What professionalism means for a broker

Professionalism is the standard of conduct expected of someone who holds a position of trust and expertise that clients rely on for decisions with real financial consequences. For a RIBO-licensed broker, professionalism is not a soft or optional quality — it is the practical expression of the legal and ethical duties the licence carries.

The core components brokers are expected to demonstrate:

Accountability
Owning the outcomes of your advice and actions, including mistakes.
Competence
Only providing advice and service within your actual knowledge and licensing scope.
Honesty & integrity
Representing facts, coverage, and limitations truthfully, even when it's inconvenient.
Fairness & respect
Treating every client equitably regardless of the size of their business or premium.

WORKING UNDER SUPERVISION

As an entry-level broker, you practise under a defined period of supervision by your Principal Broker or a designated supervisor (see 1.6). This is not a formality — it means your work is subject to review, and part of professionalism at this stage is knowing when to escalate a question rather than guessing. Recognizing the limits of your own competence and consulting your supervisor is itself a professional obligation, not a weakness.

Remember "I didn't know" is rarely a full defence for a professional. Professionalism includes knowing what you don't know, and asking before acting rather than after a client is harmed.

2.2 RIBO Code of Conduct

Core principles and professional obligations

The RIBO Code of Conduct sets out the professional obligations every licensed broker must follow in dealing with clients, insurers, and the public. It builds directly on the Principles of Conduct introduced in Chapter 1 (see 1.8) and translates them into specific, practical expectations.

PrincipleWhat it requires in practice
Honesty & integrityAccurate representation of coverage, limitations, and facts — no exaggeration, no omission designed to close a sale.
DisclosureProactively telling clients what they need to know to make an informed decision, including conflicts and compensation (see 2.5, 2.6).
ConfidentialityProtecting client information from unauthorized use or disclosure (this connects directly to PIPEDA — see 2.10).
CompetenceActing only within your actual knowledge, training, and licence category; escalating when a matter exceeds your competence.
Conflicts of interestIdentifying and properly disclosing situations where your interests could compete with the client's (see 2.5).
ComplianceFollowing RIBO's Regulations, By-Laws, and all applicable law in the course of practice.
Important The RIBO Code of Conduct is not aspirational language — a breach of these principles is the basis for RIBO complaints, investigations, and discipline (see 1.6). Client treatment, honesty, and disclosure failures are among the most common sources of RIBO complaints against entry-level licensees.

2.3 Fair Treatment of Consumers

What "fair treatment" requires

Fair treatment of consumers is a specific, structured expectation — not just general politeness. It means genuinely centering the client's interests through:

  • Clear communication — explaining coverage, cost, and process in language the client actually understands, not just technically accurate jargon.
  • Appropriate advice — recommendations that fit the client's actual needs and risk profile, not the easiest sale.
  • Transparency — being upfront about what a policy does and does not do, including limitations that might make a client hesitant.
  • Avoiding misleading information — never allowing a client to reasonably believe something about their coverage that isn't true.
  • Accessibility — ensuring communication and service are genuinely accessible to the client, adapting as needed.

CONSUMER VULNERABILITY

Some clients — due to age, health, language barriers, financial stress, or unfamiliarity with insurance — are more vulnerable to being misled, overwhelmed, or pressured. Fair treatment obligations apply with heightened care in these situations: a broker should slow down, confirm understanding more deliberately, and avoid any appearance of exploiting a client's vulnerability to close a sale or avoid a difficult conversation.

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

An elderly client, clearly confused about the difference between two policy options, tells you "just pick whichever one is easier for you to set up." One option pays you a slightly higher commission.

Not appropriate. A vague "you decide" from a confused client is not informed consent — it's a sign the client needs a clearer explanation, not an invitation to prioritize your own compensation.
Correct approach. Fair treatment requires ensuring the client actually understands their options before a decision is made on their behalf — especially with a vulnerable client. The broker's compensation must never influence which option is recommended or selected.
Not appropriate. Optimizing for the broker's convenience rather than the client's actual needs is the opposite of fair treatment, even if no dishonesty is directly involved.

2.4 CISRO Code of Conduct for Insurance Intermediaries

Purpose and key principles

The CISRO Code of Conduct for Insurance Intermediaries is a harmonized set of conduct standards developed by CISRO (see 1.7) for use by member regulators — including RIBO — in setting expectations for licensed intermediaries across Canada.

Purpose: to provide consistent baseline conduct expectations for insurance intermediaries regardless of province, supporting public confidence in the intermediary channel nationally.

Key principles largely mirror and reinforce what the RIBO Code of Conduct already requires of Ontario brokers:

  • Intermediary responsibilities — acting with skill, care, and diligence appropriate to a licensed professional.
  • Fair treatment — the same consumer-centered standard covered in 2.3.
  • Conflicts — identifying and appropriately managing conflicts of interest.
  • Disclosure — proactive, honest disclosure to clients of information relevant to their decision.
  • Professional conduct — maintaining the reputation and integrity of the profession in all client and public interactions.
Important — how CISRO relates to RIBO CISRO does not license or discipline individual Ontario brokers — that remains RIBO's role. CISRO's Code functions as a harmonizing reference point that informs and aligns with RIBO's own standards. Think of it as the national-level conduct framework that RIBO's Ontario-specific Code of Conduct reflects and builds on.

2.5 Conflicts of Interest

Common conflict types

For each conflict type below: identify the conflict, understand why it matters, and know what disclosure and conduct is required.

Fees and commissions▸

The conflict: A broker may be paid differently depending on which insurer or product is selected, creating a financial incentive that may not align with the client's best option.

Why it matters: A client trusts the broker's recommendation to be about their needs, not the broker's compensation.

Disclosure requirement: Clients should understand that the broker is compensated (typically via commission) and, where a conflict genuinely exists, that fact should be disclosed.

Appropriate conduct: Recommendations must be driven by client needs and suitability, not the size of the commission.

Ownership interests▸

The conflict: A broker or brokerage with an ownership stake in an insurer, MGA, or related business has a financial incentive to direct business there.

Why it matters: The client may not know the "recommendation" also benefits the broker's own ownership position.

Disclosure requirement: Any ownership relationship that could reasonably affect the recommendation must be disclosed to the client.

Referral arrangements▸

The conflict: A broker who refers a client to, or receives referrals from, another business may have a financial incentive tied to the referral rather than the client's interest (see also 2.7).

Disclosure requirement: Referral relationships and any associated compensation must be disclosed.

Personal interests▸

The conflict: A personal relationship with a client (family, friend) or a personal stake in the outcome can cloud objective advice.

Appropriate conduct: Recognize when a personal relationship could affect judgment, disclose it, and maintain the same professional standard regardless of the relationship.

Insurer relationships▸

The conflict: A brokerage's volume-based contracts or closer relationships with certain insurers can create pressure to place business with a "preferred" market even when it isn't the best fit for the client.

Appropriate conduct: Market access relationships should never override a genuine best-fit analysis for the client.

Exam Tip Exam scenarios often test whether you can tell the difference between an actual conflict (a real, current competing interest) and a potential conflict (a situation that could become a conflict depending on circumstances). Both generally require disclosure — the point of the question is usually whether you correctly identify that a conflict exists at all, not whether it's already caused harm.

2.6 Disclosure

What must be disclosed, and when

CategoryWhat must be disclosed
FeesAny fees charged to the client beyond standard premium (e.g., broker fees where applicable).
CompensationThat the broker is compensated (commission structure) for placing business, especially where it could reasonably influence a recommendation.
ConflictsAny actual or potential conflict of interest, as covered in 2.5.
Material informationCoverage limitations, exclusions, and anything that would affect the client's decision to proceed.

When disclosure is required: Generally, disclosure should happen before or at the point a client is making a decision — not after the fact, and not only if the client happens to ask.

Client understanding: Disclosure is not satisfied merely by providing information — the broker should take reasonable steps to confirm the client actually understood what was disclosed, particularly for complex or consequential information.

Documentation: Disclosures made (and the client's acknowledgment where applicable) should be documented in the client file — this protects both the client's ability to make an informed decision and the broker's own E&O position (see 8.6, 9.14).

Warning Disclosure buried in fine print or a document the client is unlikely to read does not meet the spirit of the disclosure obligation. The standard is genuine, practical understanding — not technical compliance.

2.7 Referral Fees

Referral arrangements and their rules

A referral fee is compensation paid for directing a client to another business or professional (or received for a client referred to you). Referral arrangements are common in practice — a broker might refer a client to a mortgage professional, or receive client referrals from a car dealership — but they carry specific disclosure and regulatory considerations.

  • Who may/may not receive referral fees: Whether a referral fee can be paid or received depends on the nature of the arrangement and applicable regulatory requirements; not every party in a referral relationship is necessarily eligible to receive compensation for it.
  • Disclosure requirements: A client should be told when a referral fee arrangement exists that is relevant to the service they're receiving, so they understand any potential influence on the recommendation.
  • Regulatory considerations: Referral arrangements must not function as a way to circumvent licensing requirements (e.g., an unlicensed party effectively acting as an insurance intermediary through a referral fee structure).
  • Ethical considerations: A referral should be made because it's genuinely useful to the client, not solely because it generates a fee.
RIBO Exam Trap Referral fees are easy to confuse with ordinary commission. A referral fee is compensation for the introduction, not for placing or servicing the insurance itself — but it still triggers the same disclosure principle: the client should know about compensation arrangements that could affect the advice or relationship they're receiving.

2.8 Consent

Express vs. implied consent

Express consent is clearly and affirmatively given — verbally, in writing, or electronically — leaving no ambiguity that the client agreed. Example: a client signs a form explicitly agreeing to a policy change, or clicks "I agree" to receive electronic communications.

Implied consent is inferred from the circumstances and conduct of the relationship rather than a direct statement — for example, a client who has an existing business relationship with the brokerage may be understood to consent to certain related communications, within reasonable and limited bounds. Implied consent has real limits and cannot be stretched to cover everything a broker might want to do.

WHEN CONSENT IS REQUIRED

Consent is required, among other situations, for: collecting and using personal information beyond what's necessary for the immediate transaction (PIPEDA — see 2.10); sending certain electronic communications (CASL — see 2.11); and making certain policy changes or actions on the client's behalf.

DOCUMENTATION AND WITHDRAWAL

Consent obtained should be documented in the client file. Clients also generally have the right to withdraw consent — for example, unsubscribing from electronic marketing, or revoking consent for a particular use of their personal information — and a broker must respect that withdrawal going forward.

Quick check: can implied consent be used to justify adding a client to a marketing email list they never asked about?

Generally no — this stretches implied consent beyond its reasonable bounds. Electronic marketing communications are specifically governed by CASL (see 2.11), which has its own, stricter consent framework. An existing business relationship does not automatically imply consent to unrelated marketing communications.

2.9 Negative Billing

What it is and why it matters

Negative billing refers to the practice of automatically renewing, adding, or changing a client's coverage (and billing them for it) without the client's clear, affirmative consent — placing the burden on the client to actively opt out rather than requiring them to opt in.

Example: A brokerage automatically adds an optional coverage enhancement to a renewal and bills the client for it, expecting the client to notice and object if they don't want it, rather than confirming the addition with the client first.

CONSUMER IMPLICATIONS

Negative billing shifts the burden of decision-making away from the client and undermines informed consent — a client may pay for coverage they never actually agreed to, simply because they didn't notice or object in time.

REGULATORY CONSIDERATIONS

Negative billing practices sit in tension with the fair treatment and disclosure/consent obligations covered throughout this chapter. A broker should ensure clients are actively informed of, and agree to, changes that affect their coverage and premium — not simply notified after the fact with an opt-out option buried in a renewal package.

Warning Even where a practice is not absolutely prohibited in every circumstance, negative billing is a significant fair-treatment and consent risk area. When in doubt, confirm affirmatively with the client rather than relying on their silence as agreement.

2.10 Privacy (PIPEDA)

What PIPEDA governs

The Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA) is the federal law governing how private-sector organizations — including insurance brokerages — collect, use, and disclose personal information in the course of commercial activity. Because a broker's business inherently involves handling sensitive personal and financial information, PIPEDA principles run through nearly every client interaction.

PrincipleWhat it requires
CollectionOnly collect personal information that is genuinely necessary for the identified purpose (e.g., underwriting, policy servicing) — not information collected "just in case."
UseUse personal information only for the purpose it was collected for, unless further consent is obtained.
DisclosureOnly disclose personal information to third parties (e.g., an insurer, another broker) as necessary and with appropriate consent/authority.
ConsentIndividuals must generally consent to the collection, use, and disclosure of their personal information, with the type of consent (express/implied) appropriate to the sensitivity of the information.
Storage & securityPersonal information must be stored securely, with appropriate safeguards against unauthorized access.
AccessIndividuals generally have the right to access the personal information an organization holds about them.
AccuracyPersonal information used to make decisions about an individual should be kept accurate and up to date.
RetentionPersonal information should not be kept indefinitely — only as long as necessary for the purpose it was collected, subject to other legal/regulatory retention requirements (e.g., RIBO record-keeping — see 11.5).
Breach / accidental disclosureOrganizations must have processes to detect, respond to, and where required, report breaches or accidental disclosures of personal information.
Exam Tip PIPEDA questions often test whether you recognize a violation pattern: collecting more information than needed, using it for an unrelated purpose without new consent, or disclosing it to a third party without authority. Look for the specific principle being violated rather than just "this seems like a privacy problem."
WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

A colleague at another brokerage — a personal friend — asks you to look up a mutual acquaintance's policy details "just out of curiosity," with no business reason.

Not appropriate. "Harmless curiosity" is not a lawful purpose under PIPEDA, and there is no client consent for this disclosure. This is exactly the kind of casual, well-intentioned request that leads to real privacy breaches.
Correct approach. Personal information can only be used or disclosed for the purpose it was collected for, with appropriate consent. Personal curiosity from a friend, even without malicious intent, is not a lawful basis for disclosure.

2.11 CASL

Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation

CASL regulates the sending of commercial electronic messages (CEMs) — emails, texts, and other electronic messages that encourage participation in a commercial activity, including marketing and promotional communications from a brokerage to clients or prospects.

CONSENT UNDER CASL

CASL generally requires consent (express or, in narrower circumstances, implied) before sending a commercial electronic message. Express consent is the stronger and more broadly reliable basis; implied consent under CASL is narrower and more time-limited than the general implied-consent concept discussed in 2.8 — it typically depends on an existing, recent business relationship, and does not last indefinitely.

UNSUBSCRIBE REQUIREMENTS

Every commercial electronic message sent under CASL must include a functioning, easy-to-use unsubscribe mechanism, and any unsubscribe request must be honoured promptly.

ELECTRONIC MARKETING AND BROKER/CLIENT COMMUNICATION

Not every message a broker sends is a "commercial electronic message" under CASL — routine service communications about an existing client's own policy (a renewal notice, a claims update) are generally treated differently from marketing communications promoting new products or services. Brokers need to distinguish between the two, since CASL's consent and unsubscribe requirements are specifically aimed at the marketing/promotional category.

Important — CASL vs. PIPEDA These are related but distinct laws. PIPEDA governs the collection, use, and disclosure of personal information generally. CASL specifically governs sending commercial electronic messages. A broker can be fully PIPEDA-compliant in how they store a client's email address, and still violate CASL by sending marketing messages without proper consent or an unsubscribe option.
Quick check: does a renewal reminder for a client's existing policy require CASL marketing consent?

Generally, a routine service communication tied to a client's existing policy (like a renewal reminder) is treated differently from promotional marketing content, and would not typically require the same CASL marketing consent. However, if that same message also promotes a new, unrelated product, the promotional portion may bring CASL's requirements into play. When communications combine service and marketing content, treat the stricter marketing rules as the safer standard to follow.

2.12 Ethical Decision-Making

Handling ethical scenarios systematically

Entry-level brokers will face situations involving conflicts of interest, privacy questions, misrepresentation, pressure from a client or employer, suspected fraud, incomplete information, or a possible regulatory violation. The exam tests whether you can identify the right response pattern — not just recite rules in isolation.

A GENERAL APPROACH

  1. Identify exactly what the ethical issue is (a conflict? a disclosure gap? potential fraud?).
  2. Consider your obligations to the client, the insurer, and the regulator — these don't always point the same direction, and part of professionalism is recognizing the tension.
  3. Determine whether you have the authority and competence to resolve it yourself, or whether it needs escalation.
  4. Escalate appropriately — to a supervisor, the Principal Broker, your employer, or, where warranted, RIBO, FSRA, or another appropriate authority.
  5. Document what happened and how it was handled.

WHO TO ESCALATE TO, AND WHEN

SituationTypical escalation path
Uncertain how to handle a client situation within normal practiceSupervisor or Principal Broker
Suspected internal misconduct or employer pressure to act improperlyPrincipal Broker (or, if the Principal Broker is implicated, directly to RIBO)
Suspected client or third-party fraudSupervisor/Principal Broker, insurer, and potentially RIBO depending on severity
Suspected regulatory violation at the brokerage levelRIBO, and FSRA if it involves market conduct or auto insurance regulatory matters
RIBO Exam Trap Exam scenarios frequently include "employer pressure" as a distractor — the correct answer is almost never to comply with an instruction that would violate your professional or regulatory obligations, even when it comes from a supervisor or the Principal Broker. Your licence and professional obligations are personal to you.

Applied scenario

WHAT WOULD YOU DO?

Your supervisor tells you to leave a client's prior claims history off a new application "since it happened a long time ago and probably won't matter." You're not sure this is correct.

Not appropriate. Supervisory authority does not override your own professional and regulatory obligations. Prior claims history is generally a material fact regardless of age, and omitting it is a disclosure/misrepresentation problem (see 1.2, 1.10, 8.8) that exposes both you and the client.
Correct approach. Voice the concern directly and explain the regulatory basis for it. If the instruction persists despite a reasonable, professional explanation, escalate further — this is exactly the kind of situation the escalation chain (Principal Broker, RIBO) exists for.
Partially better, but incomplete. Quietly correcting the problem without addressing it directly avoids an immediate conflict, but it doesn't address the underlying issue — your supervisor may repeat the same instruction with the next client, and the concern should still be raised and documented.

Key Takeaways

  • Professionalism translates a broker's legal and ethical duties into daily practice: accountability, competence, honesty, fairness, and recognizing the limits of your own knowledge while under supervision.
  • The RIBO Code of Conduct and the CISRO Code of Conduct reinforce the same core principles — honesty, disclosure, confidentiality, competence, conflicts of interest, and compliance — with CISRO providing a harmonized national reference point.
  • Fair treatment of consumers means genuinely centering client understanding and interests, with extra care for vulnerable clients.
  • Conflicts of interest (fees/commissions, ownership, referrals, personal interests, insurer relationships) require identification and disclosure — both actual and potential conflicts matter.
  • Disclosure must produce genuine client understanding, not just technical delivery of information, and should be documented.
  • Referral fees require disclosure and must not be used to circumvent licensing requirements.
  • Express consent is clear and affirmative; implied consent is narrower and context-dependent — CASL's implied consent is even narrower than general implied consent.
  • Negative billing (auto-adding/billing coverage without affirmative client agreement) is a fair-treatment and consent risk.
  • PIPEDA governs personal information collection, use, disclosure, storage, access, accuracy, retention, and breach response.
  • CASL specifically governs commercial electronic messages — consent and mandatory unsubscribe mechanisms — and is distinct from PIPEDA.
  • Ethical decision-making follows a structured approach: identify the issue, weigh competing obligations, determine your authority to resolve it, escalate appropriately, and document. Supervisory or employer pressure never overrides your own regulatory obligations.

Practice Questions

KNOWLEDGE · DEFINITION · EASY1. Which federal law specifically governs the sending of commercial electronic messages, including consent and unsubscribe requirements?
Correct answer: B. CASL (Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation) specifically regulates commercial electronic messages. PIPEDA governs personal information handling more broadly, and the other two options are Ontario-specific insurance statutes unrelated to electronic messaging.
COMPREHENSION · ETHICS · MODERATE2. A broker discloses to a client that they receive a commission for placing insurance, but does not mention that one particular insurer pays a notably higher commission rate than others. Is this adequate disclosure?
Correct answer: B. General commission disclosure is a good start, but a conflict of interest requires disclosure specific enough for the client to actually understand what could influence the recommendation — a meaningfully higher payout from one insurer is exactly this kind of conflict.
APPLICATION · SCENARIO · DIFFICULT3. A client's information was accidentally emailed to the wrong recipient by a brokerage employee. What does this situation primarily engage?
Correct answer: B. An accidental disclosure of personal information directly engages PIPEDA's breach/accidental disclosure principle, which requires organizations to have processes to detect, respond to, and where required, report such incidents. The other options are unrelated to this fact pattern.
APPLICATION · ETHICS · DIFFICULT4. Your Principal Broker instructs you to omit a client's material claims history from a new application. You've already raised the concern once and been told to proceed anyway. What is the most appropriate next step?
Correct answer: C. Your RIBO licence and professional obligations are personal to you and are not overridden by an employer or supervisor's instruction — even the Principal Broker. Where an internal escalation has already failed to resolve a clear misrepresentation/disclosure problem, escalating externally (to RIBO) is the appropriate next step.
KNOWLEDGE · COMPARISON · EASY5. What distinguishes negative billing from a standard policy renewal at existing terms?
Correct answer: B. Negative billing specifically involves a change (added coverage, increased cost) billed without the client's clear agreement — placing the burden on the client to notice and object, rather than requiring an opt-in.
